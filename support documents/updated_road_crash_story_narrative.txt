WHO GETS HURT ON AUSTRALIAN ROADS — AND WHY THE BURDEN IS STILL RISING
A Data Story on Hospitalised Injuries from Road Crashes in Australia, 2011–2021
================================================================================


OPENING — THE NATIONAL PICTURE
--------------------------------

Every year in Australia, tens of thousands of people are admitted to hospital because of a road crash. These are not fatality counts. They represent people who survived the crash but still required hospital care. This makes hospitalised injury data an important measure of the broader road safety burden: the injuries that do not always appear in headline road toll figures, but still affect individuals, families, hospitals, and long-term recovery.

The latest transformed dataset shows that Australia recorded 403,293 hospitalised road crash cases across states and territories between 2011 and 2021, with a total of 1,970,095 bed days. This is a much larger burden than the earlier unprocessed narrative suggested.

The national pattern is not stable. In 2011, there were 33,553 hospitalised road crash cases. By 2019, the annual total had risen to 38,869. That represents an increase of about 15.8 percent across eight years. The growth was not perfectly smooth, but the direction was clear: more Australians were ending up in hospital after road crashes by the end of the decade than at the beginning.

Then came 2020. Hospitalisations fell to 37,292 cases, interrupting the upward trend. This decline is consistent with the broader COVID-19 period, when travel behaviour changed and road exposure was reduced. However, the fall did not signal a structural solution to road trauma. By 2021, hospitalisations increased again to 39,035 cases, the highest annual total in the transformed state-level dataset.

This is where the story begins: not with a single crash, but with a decade-long pattern of rising hospital demand. The data suggests that road safety should not only be judged by deaths. Hospitalisations reveal a wider and persistent form of harm: serious injuries that continue to grow even when vehicles, enforcement, and safety campaigns improve.


CHAPTER 2 — WHERE
-------------------

Australia is not one road system. It is eight jurisdictions with different geographies, population densities, urban layouts, rural road networks, and travel behaviours. When the national trend is broken down by state and territory, the burden becomes uneven.

New South Wales recorded the highest total number of hospitalised road crash cases across the decade, with 116,060 cases from 2011 to 2021. Victoria followed with 107,942 cases, and Queensland followed with 93,967 cases. Together, these three states accounted for most of the hospitalised injury burden in raw numbers. This is expected because they are among Australia's most populous states, but their trends are not identical.

New South Wales started the decade with 10,618 cases in 2011, but by 2021 the annual count had fallen to 9,003. This represents a decrease of about 15.2 percent. In contrast, Victoria increased from 9,326 cases in 2011 to 11,130 in 2021, a rise of about 19.3 percent. Victoria overtook New South Wales in the later part of the decade and became the highest-count state in 2021.

Queensland shows the strongest absolute growth among the large states. Its hospitalised road crash cases increased from 6,322 in 2011 to 10,447 in 2021. This is a rise of about 65.2 percent, making Queensland one of the clearest examples of a worsening state-level trend in the transformed dataset.

The smaller jurisdictions have lower raw counts, but raw counts alone can be misleading. Tasmania, the Northern Territory, and the Australian Capital Territory all record far fewer cases than the larger states, yet their population sizes are also much smaller. Tasmania increased from 493 cases in 2011 to 805 in 2021. The Northern Territory increased from 456 to 710, and the ACT increased from 537 to 740.

The Northern Territory is especially important when population is considered. In 2021, the NT recorded 710 cases among a population of about 249,300 people, or roughly 285 hospitalised cases per 100,000 people. This was the highest per-capita burden in the transformed dataset. Queensland followed at about 198 cases per 100,000 people, while New South Wales was much lower at about 111 cases per 100,000 people.

This means that the largest raw burden sits in the eastern states, but the most intense population-adjusted burden sits in the Northern Territory. A national chart based only on total cases would make the NT look small. A rate-based view shows that its road injury burden is proportionally severe.

The geographic story is therefore two-sided. New South Wales, Victoria, and Queensland carry the largest number of hospitalised injuries. But remote and smaller jurisdictions, especially the Northern Territory, carry a heavier burden relative to population. This distinction matters because policy responses based only on raw totals may overlook communities where road trauma is proportionally most severe.


CHAPTER 3 — WHO
-----------------

Behind every hospitalisation is a person. The transformed dataset allows the story to be examined by age and sex, and the corrected results change several claims from the earlier narrative.

By age, the largest group is people aged 40 to 64. Across the decade, this group recorded 132,422 hospitalised cases, or about 32.9 percent of age-grouped cases. The next largest group was people aged 26 to 39, with 96,742 cases, followed by people aged 17 to 25, with 86,016 cases.

This means the highest volume of hospitalised road crash injuries is concentrated among working-age Australians. This is likely connected to exposure: people in these age groups are active road users for work, family, commuting, and daily travel. The data does not prove why they are injured more often, but it clearly shows that they make up the largest share of hospitalised cases.

Severity tells a different story. When severity is approximated by average hospital bed days per case, older Australians experience longer hospital stays. People aged 75 and over averaged about 9.9 bed days per case, the highest of all age groups. People aged 65 to 74 averaged about 7.3 bed days. By comparison, people aged 26 to 39 averaged about 4.1 bed days, and people aged 17 to 25 averaged about 3.9 bed days.

This creates an important distinction between volume and severity. Middle-aged adults are hospitalised most often, but older adults stay in hospital longer when they are injured. As Australia's population ages, this severity pattern becomes increasingly important for healthcare planning because older road users may require more hospital resources per crash injury.

Sex also needed correction. The earlier narrative incorrectly stated that females account for the majority of hospitalised road crash cases. The transformed dataset shows the opposite. Across 2011 to 2021, males accounted for 262,280 cases, or about 65.0 percent of sex-grouped hospitalised injuries. Females accounted for 141,000 cases, or about 35.0 percent.

This male majority is consistent across every year in the dataset. In 2011, males represented about 65.7 percent of hospitalised cases. In 2021, they represented about 65.2 percent. The share fluctuated slightly, but the overall pattern remained stable: males were consistently overrepresented in hospitalised road crash injuries.

Average bed days by sex are closer than the case counts. Males averaged about 5.1 bed days per case, while females averaged about 4.6 bed days. This suggests that males carry both the larger volume of hospitalised injuries and a slightly higher average hospital stay in the transformed dataset.

The demographic story is therefore clear: working-age adults account for the largest number of hospitalised cases, older adults experience the longest average hospital stays, and males are consistently the majority of hospitalised road crash patients.


CHAPTER 4 — HOW
-----------------

Knowing who gets hurt tells us about the people. Knowing how they get hurt tells us about the road system and the types of crashes that create the greatest hospital burden.

The largest road user category in the transformed state_road dataset is car driver, passenger, or unknown position. This combined category recorded 193,216 cases across the decade, making it the dominant source of hospitalised road crash injuries. This is expected because cars are the most common form of road travel in Australia.

However, vulnerable road users also represent a major share of the hospital burden. Motorcyclists recorded 91,042 cases, and pedal cyclists recorded 74,020 cases. These are not minor categories. Together, motorcyclists and pedal cyclists accounted for more than 165,000 hospitalised cases across the decade.

Pedestrians recorded fewer cases, with 27,806 hospitalisations, but they experienced the most severe outcomes by average bed days. Pedestrians averaged about 9.1 bed days per case, the highest of all road user groups. Motorcyclists averaged about 5.5 bed days per case, while car occupants averaged about 4.7 bed days. Pedal cyclists averaged about 2.9 bed days.

This means that the most common hospitalised road user group is car occupants, but the most severe average hospital stay belongs to pedestrians. The distinction is important. A chart based only on counts highlights cars and motorcycles. A chart based on average bed days highlights pedestrians as a smaller but more severe group.

The counterparty data adds another layer. The most common counterparty category was car, pick-up truck, or van, with 166,457 cases. This reflects the reality that many serious road injuries occur in interactions with ordinary passenger vehicles. Non-collision transport accidents were also highly significant, with 106,112 cases. These include events where the injury did not involve a direct collision with another moving vehicle. Fixed or stationary objects accounted for 57,561 cases, while the broader 'Other' category accounted for 73,163 cases.

Severity again changes the interpretation. Crashes involving fixed or stationary objects averaged about 6.3 bed days per case, higher than car, pick-up truck, or van counterparties at about 5.1 days and non-collision transport accidents at about 3.8 days. This suggests that fixed-object crashes are not the largest category by count, but they are relatively severe in terms of hospital stay.

There are also geographic differences in crash type. Victoria and New South Wales had higher proportions of cases involving cars, pick-up trucks, or vans, at about 46.5 percent and 43.4 percent respectively. The Northern Territory had a much higher proportion of non-collision transport accidents, at about 44.4 percent of its counterparty-classified cases. Tasmania and the ACT also had relatively high non-collision shares, above 33 percent.

This pattern suggests that dense urban road systems and remote or regional road systems create different hospitalisation profiles. Urban states show a stronger multi-vehicle pattern. The Northern Territory shows a stronger non-collision pattern, which may reflect long-distance travel, remote roads, and different exposure conditions. The dataset does not directly explain the causes, but it shows that the type of crash burden differs by jurisdiction.


CLOSING — CONVERGENCE
-----------------------

The five dimensions of this data story — trend, geography, age, sex, and crash type — do not describe separate problems. They describe one connected road injury system.

Nationally, hospitalised road crash cases increased from 33,553 in 2011 to 39,035 in 2021. The 2020 decline was temporary, and the 2021 rebound brought the annual count to the highest level in the transformed state-level dataset. This shows that the hospital burden of road crashes remained persistent and growing across the decade.

Geographically, the largest raw burdens were concentrated in New South Wales, Victoria, and Queensland. But the fastest growth was especially visible in Queensland, Tasmania, and the Northern Territory. When population is considered, the Northern Territory stands out as the highest-burden jurisdiction in 2021, even though its raw case count is small.

Demographically, the main case burden falls on working-age adults and males. People aged 40 to 64 recorded the largest number of cases, while males made up about 65 percent of hospitalised injuries. But severity points toward older Australians, especially those aged 75 and over, who had the longest average hospital stays.

By road user type, car occupants recorded the largest number of hospitalisations, but pedestrians had the longest average hospital stays. Motorcyclists and pedal cyclists also represented major hospitalised injury groups, confirming that vulnerable and less-protected road users remain central to the road trauma story.

By crash type, collisions involving cars, pick-up trucks, or vans were the largest counterparty category, while fixed-object crashes produced the longest average stays. Non-collision transport accidents were especially important in the Northern Territory, showing that remote and regional road conditions may create different injury patterns from dense urban road networks.

If this data story were to inform one policy question, it would be: which groups and places are being hidden by national averages? The answer is clear. Large states carry the biggest hospital workload, but smaller and remote jurisdictions can carry the highest proportional burden. Working-age males account for the most cases, but older Australians and pedestrians face more severe hospital stays. Car occupants dominate the counts, but motorcyclists, cyclists, and pedestrians remain highly exposed.

Road safety analysis should therefore not stop at fatalities or total case counts. Hospitalisation data shows a broader and more persistent form of harm. It reveals where the healthcare burden is growing, which groups are most exposed, and which injuries demand longer recovery. Understanding these patterns is the first step toward designing road safety responses that match the actual burden shown in the data.


================================================================================
DATA NOTES AND LIMITATIONS

All figures in this revised narrative are drawn from the latest transformed_datasets.xlsx workbook supplied for the project. The main state-level statistics use the state_summary sheet. Demographic statistics use the state_age and state_sex sheets. Road user statistics use the state_road sheet. Counterparty statistics use the state_counterparty sheet. Population-adjusted context uses the population sheet.

The narrative uses hospitalised road crash case counts and bed days. Average bed days are calculated as total bed days divided by total count of cases for the relevant group. This is a practical measure of hospital stay length, not a clinical severity score.

Most comparisons are based on raw counts. Raw counts are useful for understanding hospital workload, but they do not automatically show risk because states and territories have different population sizes, road lengths, travel volumes, and demographic structures. Where per-capita context is included, it is used only as supporting context.

Some categories are combined in the transformed dataset. For example, the main state_road sheet groups car driver, passenger, or unknown position into one category. Therefore, this revised narrative does not separate car drivers from car passengers unless the transformed workbook provides that separation.
