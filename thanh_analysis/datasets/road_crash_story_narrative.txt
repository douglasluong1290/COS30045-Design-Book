WHO GETS HURT ON AUSTRALIAN ROADS — AND WHY IT KEEPS GETTING WORSE
A Data Story on Hospitalised Injuries from Road Crashes in Australia, 2011–2021
================================================================================


OPENING — THE NATIONAL PICTURE
--------------------------------

Every year in Australia, tens of thousands of people are admitted to hospital
because of a road crash. Not killed — hospitalised. This is the quieter,
larger side of the road toll that rarely makes headlines, yet it places an
enormous and growing burden on individuals, families, and the healthcare system.

Between 2011 and 2021, Australia recorded over 190,000 hospitalised road crash
injuries. That is not a stable figure. It grew — almost every single year.

In 2011, approximately 16,400 people were hospitalised following a road crash.
By 2019, that number had climbed to over 19,500 — a rise of nearly 20 percent
across just eight years. Road safety campaigns ran. Speed limits were enforced.
Safer cars were sold. And yet, more people ended up in hospital each year than
the year before.

Then came 2020. For the first time in nearly a decade, hospitalisations fell —
dropping back to around 18,600. COVID-19 lockdowns emptied Australian roads.
With fewer vehicles moving, fewer people were injured. It was an accidental
experiment that confirmed what the data already suggested: the rising trend was
being driven by behaviour and exposure, not by any structural improvement in
how safe our roads had become.

The experiment did not last. By 2021, as restrictions eased and traffic
returned, hospitalisations rebounded to their highest recorded level — over
20,000 cases in a single year. The system had not changed. It had merely been
paused.

This is where our story begins. Not with a single crash, but with a decade-long
pattern that tells us something important about who is using Australian roads,
how they are being hurt, and which communities are carrying the heaviest burden.


CHAPTER 2 — WHERE
-------------------

Australia is not one road system. It is eight jurisdictions with different
geographies, population densities, urban layouts, and driving cultures. When
we break the national trend down by state and territory, the picture becomes
considerably more interesting — and considerably more unequal.

New South Wales and Victoria together account for the majority of hospitalised
road crash cases across the decade, which is unsurprising given that they are
Australia's two most populous states. But the relationship between the two
tells a story of its own.

In 2011, NSW led Victoria in total hospitalisations. Through the mid-2010s the
gap narrowed. By 2018, Victoria had overtaken New South Wales and has largely
stayed ahead since — despite the two states having broadly comparable
population sizes. Something about the way Victorians use roads, or the roads
themselves, produced a quietly worsening outcome relative to its closest
comparator.

Queensland presents the starkest absolute trend of any state. From around 6,300
cases in 2011, Queensland's hospitalisation count climbed steadily to over
10,400 by 2021 — a rise of approximately 65 percent over the decade. No other
state comes close to that rate of increase. Whether this reflects population
growth, changing road use patterns, or infrastructure pressures, the trajectory
demands attention.

At the other end of the scale sit the smaller jurisdictions — Tasmania, the
Australian Capital Territory, and the Northern Territory — which register far
lower absolute counts simply because far fewer people live there. But raw
counts can deceive.

The Northern Territory, in particular, warrants careful reading. Its numbers
appear small on a national chart. Yet the NT has a population of under 300,000
people, and its hospitalisation counts — consistently between 400 and 800 cases
per year — represent an injury burden that, relative to population, far exceeds
that of any other jurisdiction. The NT's road network is vast, remote, and
heavily used by communities with limited access to alternative transport. A
single crash in a remote area can mean hours before an ambulance arrives and
days before rehabilitation begins.

It is important to be transparent about what this data can and cannot tell us.
These are raw counts. A direct comparison of New South Wales with the Northern
Territory based on case numbers alone would be misleading. Population size,
road kilometres, traffic volumes, and demographic composition all differ
enormously. Where population data allows approximate per-capita framing, it is
offered as context — but the primary dataset does not normalise for population,
and readers should interpret geographic comparisons with that limitation in mind.

What the state data does tell us clearly is this: the burden of road crash
hospitalisations is geographically concentrated, unevenly distributed, and
growing fastest in states that are already among the highest-burden jurisdictions.


CHAPTER 3 — WHO
-----------------

Behind every hospitalisation is a person. The data allows us to look at two
fundamental demographic dimensions — age and sex — and what emerges challenges
some common assumptions about who road crashes actually harm.

Start with age. The largest single group in terms of hospitalisation volume is
people aged 40 to 64, who account for approximately one in three cases across
the entire decade. This is partly a reflection of how many people in this age
group are on the road and how many kilometres they drive. The 26 to 39 group
follows, with younger adults aged 17 to 25 contributing a smaller but still
substantial share.

But volume is only one dimension of harm. The other is severity — and when we
measure severity by the average number of days spent in hospital per case, the
picture shifts dramatically.

Australians aged 75 and over are hospitalised for an average of nearly nine
days per crash injury. Those aged 65 to 74 average around six and a half days.
Compare this to working-age adults, who average between four and five and a
half days, or children under eight, who average fewer than three days. Older
Australians are not the most frequently hospitalised group — but when they are
hospitalised, they stay longer, recover more slowly, and place a substantially
greater demand on healthcare resources per case.

As Australia's population ages, this severity gradient becomes increasingly
important. The fastest-growing segment of the driving population is also the
segment that experiences the most severe outcomes when things go wrong. This
is not a distant risk — it is already visible in the data, and it will intensify.

Now consider sex. This is where the data delivers its most counterintuitive
finding.

Across every year from 2011 to 2021, and in every state and territory,
females account for the majority of hospitalised road crash cases — consistently
around 52 to 53 percent nationally. This pattern is remarkably stable. It does
not fluctuate much from year to year, and it holds across jurisdictions.

This surprises many people. Common assumptions about road safety tend to frame
men as the higher-risk group — and for fatal crashes, that remains broadly true.
Men drive more kilometres, are overrepresented in speeding incidents, and die
on roads at higher rates. But hospitalisation tells a different story.

The likely explanation lies in passenger dynamics. A significant proportion of
hospitalised road crash cases are car passengers, not drivers. Women are
disproportionately represented among passengers relative to drivers. When a
crash occurs, both the driver and passengers may be hospitalised — and the
injury profile of passengers often differs from that of drivers. This is not
about women being worse drivers. It is about who is sitting in the seat when
the crash happens.

The combined picture from age and sex is one of hidden vulnerability. The
populations most frequently hospitalised — working-age adults, females — are
not necessarily the populations suffering the most severe outcomes. The
populations with the most severe outcomes — older Australians, particularly
older women — are less visible in volume-based analyses. A data story that only
counts cases misses half the picture.


CHAPTER 4 — HOW
-----------------

Knowing who gets hurt tells us about the people. Knowing how they get hurt
tells us about the crashes themselves — and points more directly toward what
could be done differently.

The single largest category of hospitalised road users across the decade is car
drivers, with over 135,000 cases. Car passengers add another 53,000. Together,
people travelling in cars account for well over half of all hospitalisations.
This is not surprising — cars dominate Australian road use.

But the next two categories reveal something important. Motorcyclists account
for approximately 92,000 hospitalisations across the decade, and pedal cyclists
for around 75,000. These are not marginal road users. They represent the second
and third largest groups of hospitalised road crash victims — and they are
entirely unprotected by the steel and airbags that surround car occupants.

Pedestrians, with around 29,000 cases, are fewer in number but among the most
severely injured. When we measure severity by average bed days per case,
pedestrians average 8.6 days — the highest of any road user group. Motorcyclists
average 5.8 days. Car drivers and passengers fall in the 5 to 5.5 day range.
Pedal cyclists, perhaps surprisingly, average only 3.3 days — suggesting that
while many cycling crashes lead to hospitalisation, a large proportion involve
injuries that are serious enough to require admission but not severe enough for
extended stays.

The counterparty data — what the injured person collided with, or what caused
the crash — adds another layer to this picture.

The most common crash type involves another car, pick-up truck, or van, which
accounts for the majority of cases where a counterparty is identified. This
reflects the simple reality that most travel happens on shared roads where
vehicles interact. Fixed or stationary objects — poles, barriers, trees —
account for a substantial share of cases as well, representing crashes where
the vehicle left its lane or road without involving another vehicle.

One category deserves particular attention: non-collision transport accidents,
which include rollovers and single-vehicle incidents without a collision. These
account for a very large proportion of total cases — and they are especially
prevalent in the Northern Territory and in regional and remote Queensland.
A non-collision accident on a remote highway, far from emergency services,
carries consequences that are fundamentally different from the same type of
crash on a metropolitan street. The injury may be comparable. The access to
care may not be.

Different states show meaningfully different counterparty profiles. Victoria
and NSW are dominated by multi-vehicle collisions, consistent with high-density
urban road networks. Queensland and NT show higher proportions of non-collision
and fixed-object crashes, consistent with longer rural journeys on undivided
roads at higher speeds. The type of road environment shapes not just how
crashes happen, but how severe the outcomes tend to be.


CLOSING — CONVERGENCE
-----------------------

The five dimensions of this data story — trend, geography, age, sex, and crash
type — do not describe five separate problems. They describe facets of one
interconnected system, and the data points consistently toward a set of
populations and situations that carry disproportionate risk.

Nationally, road crash hospitalisations have been rising for most of the past
decade and show no structural sign of reversing. The 2020 decline was real but
temporary — a consequence of reduced traffic, not improved safety. The 2021
rebound confirmed that the underlying system had not changed.

Geographically, the burden falls hardest on the larger eastern states in
absolute terms, with Victoria showing a particularly notable rise relative to
its population. But the most acute per-capita risk sits in remote Australia —
and the Northern Territory in particular — where smaller populations, longer
roads, fewer services, and a higher proportion of vulnerable road users combine
to produce outcomes that the raw numbers understate rather than overstate.

Demographically, the story splits along two axes. Volume — the sheer number of
people hospitalised — points toward middle-aged Australians, car drivers, and
females. Severity — how long they stay, and how much healthcare they require —
points toward older Australians, pedestrians, and motorcyclists. These are not
the same people, and addressing one group does not address the other.

In terms of crash type, multi-vehicle collisions dominate the urban landscape,
while single-vehicle and non-collision crashes define the regional and remote
experience. Motorcyclists and pedestrians are the road users most likely to
convert a crash into a prolonged hospitalisation.

If this data story were to inform a single policy question, it would be this:
who is falling through the gaps of existing road safety attention? The answer
the data suggests is older Australians whose severe injuries receive less
attention than fatalities, motorcyclists and pedestrians whose volumes are high
but whose vulnerability is often underweighted in infrastructure decisions, and
remote communities where the consequences of a crash extend far beyond the
crash itself.

Road safety in Australia has made real progress on some fronts. But
hospitalisation data tells a story that fatality statistics do not — one of
persistent, growing harm that touches every state, every age group, and every
type of road user. Understanding it in full is the first step toward
addressing it.


================================================================================
DATA NOTES AND LIMITATIONS

All hospitalisation figures are drawn from the BITRE Hospitalised Injury
Dataset (2011–2021) and represent counts of cases excluding deaths in hospital
within 30 days. Figures for some states and age/road user combinations are
suppressed (marked n.p. — not published) where counts are too small to report
without risking identification. This suppression particularly affects the
Northern Territory and Australian Capital Territory, and may result in
undercounting of the true burden in those jurisdictions.

Raw counts are used throughout this story. Direct comparisons between
jurisdictions of substantially different population sizes — particularly
comparisons involving the Northern Territory — should be interpreted with
caution. Where population-based context is offered, it draws on 2021 ABS
population estimates applied uniformly across the decade, which represents
an approximation rather than a precise per-capita rate.

Severity is approximated using average bed days per hospitalised case, derived
from the national dataset. This measure reflects average hospital stay length
and should not be interpreted as a clinical severity score.
