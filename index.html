Who gets hurt on Australian roads — and why it keeps getting worse

403,293 hospitalised road crash cases. 1.97 million bed days. A decade of BITRE data, traced from the national trend down to the individual: state by state, age by age, crash by crash. Scroll to read.

01

Opening — the national picture

Australia's road injury burden grew for eight consecutive years

2011 → 2019

Between 2011 and 2019, the number of Australians hospitalised after a road crash climbed almost every year — from 33,553 cases to 38,869. A 15.8% rise across eight years, and the trend was steady rather than smooth.

2020 — the natural experiment

Then COVID-19 lockdowns hollowed out the roads. Traffic volumes fell, and hospitalisations followed — annual cases dropped to 37,292, interrupting the upward trend. For one year, the curve broke.

2021 — highest annual total

The dip lasted exactly as long as the lockdowns did. By 2021 the count rebounded to 39,035 cases — the highest annual total in the transformed state-level dataset. The problem is structural; the 2020 reprieve was behavioural.

Chapter 2 — where

The burden is not shared equally across states

Eight states, eight trajectories

Splitting the national figure by state reveals eight very different stories. NSW recorded 116,060 cases across the decade, VIC 107,942, and QLD 93,967 — together these three carried most of the raw burden, but their trends are not identical.

The VIC / NSW crossover

NSW started the decade with 10,618 cases in 2011 and fell to 9,003 by 2021 — a 15.2% decrease. Victoria moved the other way: from 9,326 to 11,130, a 19.3% rise. Victoria overtook NSW and became the highest-count state in 2021.

Queensland's strongest climb

Queensland shows the steepest absolute growth among the large states: from 6,322 cases in 2011 to 10,447 in 2021 — a +65.2% rise. That is one of the clearest examples of a worsening state-level trend in the transformed dataset.

The Northern Territory disproportion

NT looks small in absolute terms — 710 cases in 2021 — but with a population of only ~249,300 that is roughly 285 cases per 100,000 people, the highest per-capita burden in the transformed dataset. Queensland sat at ~198 per 100k, NSW at ~111.

Largest raw burden sits in the eastern states; the most intense population-adjusted burden sits in the Northern Territory.

Chapter 3 — who

Working-age adults drive the volume; older adults stay longer

Where the volume sits

Volume concentrates in working-age Australians. The 40–64 age band recorded 132,422 hospitalised cases across the decade — about 32.9% of age-grouped cases. The 26–39 group followed at 96,742, and 17–25 at 86,016.

Severity inverts the ranking

Switch the lens to average hospital stay and the volume picture flips. The 75+ group averages 9.9 bed days per case — the highest of all bands. 65–74 averages 7.3 days; 26–39 averages 4.1; 17–25 averages 3.9. Older Australians stay longer when injured.

The gender split — correcting the earlier story

Earlier readings suggested females were the majority. The transformed dataset shows the opposite: males account for about 65% of hospitalised cases (262,280 cases over 2011–2021); females are ~35% (141,000 cases). The share is consistent across every year, fluctuating only slightly between 65.2% and 65.7%.

Why it matters

Working-age males account for the largest number of hospitalised cases, but older Australians — especially the 75+ cohort — experience the longest average hospital stays. As the population ages, this severity gradient becomes increasingly important for healthcare capacity.

Chapter 4 — how

How you travel determines how badly you are hurt

Volume: car occupants

In the transformed state_road dataset, car driver, passenger or unknown position is one combined category. It recorded 193,216 cases across the decade — the dominant source of hospitalised road crash injuries.

Severity inverts the ranking

Switch to average bed days per case and the order flips. Pedestrians average 9.1 bed days — the highest of all road user groups, despite only 27,806 cases. Motorcyclists average 5.5 days (91,042 cases); cyclists 2.9 days (74,020 cases). Smaller groups, longer stays.

The counterparty mix

The most common counterparty was car, pick-up truck or van at 166,457 cases. Non-collision transport accidents (rollovers, single-vehicle run-offs) came in at 106,112, and fixed or stationary objects at 57,561. Fixed-object crashes average 6.3 bed days — relatively severe per case.

Where non-collision dominates

The non-collision share is not evenly spread. The Northern Territory records about 44.4% of its counterparty-classified cases as non-collision; Tasmania and the ACT also sit above 33%. Urban states like NSW and VIC are dominated by multi-vehicle crashes (~43–47% car-on-car) instead.

Closing — convergence

The highest-risk profile: who, where, and in what crash

All dimensions in one frame

Pulled together — states down, road users across — the matrix shows where injury concentrates. Across the decade Australia recorded 403,293 hospitalised road crash cases and ~1.97 million bed days. The brighter the cell, the heavier the burden.

The highest-volume profile

The brightest cells gather around NSW · VIC · QLD × car driver/passenger, where raw volume is largest. Working-age males drive the count: 40–64s contributed about a third of cases, and males about 65% overall.

The highest-severity profile

Switching to average hospital stay surfaces a different cohort: older Australians and pedestrians. Pedestrians average 9.1 bed days per case; the 75+ age cohort averages 9.9. Fewer events, longer recovery, higher healthcare load per case.

Raw counts. Per-capita context only — population is yearly. NT carries the heaviest proportional burden in 2021 despite a small raw case count.

Limitations surfaced in this narrative

  • Raw counts favour populous states. NSW, VIC and QLD always look large in absolute terms. NT, TAS and ACT figures are contextualised with yearly population data in Chapter 2's drill view.
  • Average bed days is a workload measure, not a clinical severity score. It is computed as total bed days ÷ total count of cases for the relevant group, then rounded to the nearest integer.
  • Combined road-user categories. The state_road sheet groups car driver, passenger or unknown position into one category, so this narrative does not separate drivers from passengers.
  • Admission boundaries are pre-computed. The Admission column comes from the transformed workbook directly — VIC and NSW each show their own admission change points (2012 for VIC, 2017 for NSW) in Chapter 2's drill line, and the 3-phase national split appears in Chapter 1.
